I keep noticing how much easier it is to speak confidently about what has gone wrong than about what should happen next. We have lived with the problem. We can describe the unfairness, the frustration, the decisions made over our heads. The alternative is harder to describe because we have not lived in it yet. People can agree that an arrangement has failed them while imagining very different replacements. For a while, everyone cheers together. The disagreement has simply booked a later meeting.

The arguments around capitalism, communism, and social democracy make this visible. Industrial capitalism expanded production and created enormous wealth, while leaving many workers with little control over their conditions. A society could be getting richer without becoming fairer to the people doing much of its work. Opposition to that arrangement grew out of clear grievances. It also opened a much larger question about ownership and power.

Marxism offered an answer at that deeper level. Its criticism concerned the relationship between those who owned productive resources and those who had to sell their labor. Changing a few unpleasant employers would leave that relationship intact. But the goal of collective ownership still left difficult questions about how people would actually govern production and hold decision-makers accountable.

Where communist governments concentrated those decisions in a party state, removing private owners did not necessarily give ordinary people control. Later moves toward markets could therefore look like a return to the very thing the revolution had rejected. Modern social democracy developed along a different path: retaining markets and private enterprise while expanding public services, workers’ rights, and social protection. These were competing responses, rather than stops on one inevitable journey.

A familiar label can hide substantial differences. Capitalism with stronger labor rights is a different arrangement from capitalism without them. Public ownership also changes meaning depending on who controls it. Some failures expose a mistaken idea; others reveal bad incentives, concentrated power, or poor execution. It takes time to work out the difference. Unfortunately, “We are still figuring out which part failed” is a difficult election slogan.

Political campaigns can make better use of certainty. “They have failed you” gets applause. “Let me explain the implementation schedule” gives everyone time to check their phones. People can vote for the same change while expecting different things from it. After the victory, each group starts pulling the new arrangement toward its own preferences. The result can be something nobody quite promised. The next campaign then has fresh material.

Yet dissatisfaction is only one engine of change. A person can enjoy life and still want to build something extraordinary. Curiosity and ambition give them a direction; grit keeps them going through uncertainty and boring afternoons. They do not need to hate their present life or know every step in advance to pursue a possibility they find worthwhile.

Scale makes that harder. A person can choose a goal for themselves. A shared ambition requires people to negotiate their purposes. A ruler with concentrated power can impose a direction, with little guarantee that others will share its benefits. Democracy gives those others a voice. The hesitation about what comes next is partly the sound of people discovering that they want different things.

That leaves me wondering whether this is simply how human judgment works. We know an existing problem better than an imagined alternative, and choosing a direction means accepting the risk of being wrong. Some hesitation seems perfectly reasonable. But is the habit of waiting for somebody else to decide inevitable? Or is it something we have learned so thoroughly that it now feels natural?

I am interested in the possibility that part of what we call human nature is an adaptation to the institutions around us. Modern schooling is one place to look, because we spend so many formative years inside it, learning what earns approval and what causes trouble.

A school organized around the same schedule, the same assignments, and the same approved answers can teach people to work and coexist. It may give them less practice deciding what deserves their effort. Complete the task, meet the deadline, wait for the next instruction. These are useful habits. They can become a very small description of a human life.

The unsettling part is how much trust we place in that arrangement. We want children to be educated. Literacy, knowledge, and good teachers can expand their freedom enormously. Good marks and qualifications open real doors. Those successes make it easy to accept the institution’s definition of progress without asking how much room it leaves for choosing a purpose.

A student can learn to judge an idea by whether it will be accepted before asking whether it matters to them. Over time, initiative may become something to display within approved boundaries. Be curious, but stay on topic. Be original, but remember the marking scheme. There is a rather narrow kind of independence available under those conditions.

Perhaps this is how a trusted system can weaken the very will it appears to strengthen. The effort remains: years of studying, working, and meeting obligations demand real persistence. What may weaken is the confidence to choose where that effort should go. We can become accomplished at pursuing goals while feeling strangely unauthorized to set them.

When approval consistently comes from getting things right, an uncertain project can feel irresponsible. Its value has not been confirmed. Its mistakes may be visible before its purpose makes sense to anyone else. Waiting for permission becomes understandable, even when nobody is formally withholding it.

I wonder how much of that habit follows us into public life. We become experienced judges of the decisions made for us, while choosing a direction together can feel unfamiliar. A politician promising a complete answer offers something reassuring: somebody else has worked out the assignment. Our part is to decide whether we approve.

Adulthood asks us to choose a life and help shape a society. We have one small question: how will this be marked?
